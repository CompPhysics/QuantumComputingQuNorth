\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background:} Resolution refinement, the adiabatic transport of an
eigenstate from a coarse to a fine model space along a shift-controlled,
gapped path, was proposed for lattice problems in which every coarse site
is resolved into a block of fine sites.  Its implementation on the pairing
Hamiltonian was validated at the gate level against alternative inputs to
the same rodeo filter.
\textbf{Purpose:} To carry the pipeline, the benchmarks and the cost
accounting to the lattice refinement of the Hubbard model, and to establish
what controls the refinement there and what it costs against the simplest
alternatives.
\textbf{Methods:} Every stage is benchmarked against exact diagonalization
in the conserved $(N_\uparrow,N_\downarrow)$ sector for the open chain
refined from four to eight and from eight to sixteen sites and for the
$2\times2\to4\times4$ plaquette.  The Galerkin coarse model is derived in
closed form, the transported state is followed with total-spin and
point-group diagnostics, the rodeo filter is evaluated with the exact
Gaussian-ensemble formula, and all inputs --- prolonged, refined along a
projector path and along a two-local un-dimerization path, an ordinary
adiabatic ramp from the free-fermion determinant, and full and pruned
unitary coupled-cluster singles-and-doubles (UCCSD) states --- are fed to
the same second-order Trotterized filter and compared by ground-state
infidelity against controlled-NOT (CNOT) count per accepted preparation.
\textbf{Results:} The coarse model is again a Hubbard model, with
$(t,U)\to(t/2,U/2)$ in one dimension, so the hierarchy is self-similar and
can start from the filled dimer, a single basis state, reaching the eight-
and sixteen-site ground states with overlap $p>0.999$ without any
variational input.  The path conserves total spin and the lattice
symmetry, so the transport is controlled by the lowest excitation in the
symmetry sector of the target, $\Deff=0.37t$--$0.88t$, while the sector
gap collapses to $0.14t$ (chain) and $0.05t$ (sixteen sites): the
refinement time is flat in $U$ and in size, and $\Deff$ is the right width
for the filter.  A coarse state in the wrong representation has exactly
zero overlap with the target ($B_1$ singlet versus $A_2$ triplet on the
plaquette); refining the lowest coarse state of the right sector instead
gives $p=0.99997$.  At the circuit level ($U=4t$, sixteen qubits) the
ordinary adiabatic ramp from the free-fermion determinant is the cheapest
route at every target accuracy, $1.9\times10^4$, $4.0\times10^4$ and
$9.1\times10^4$ CNOTs for infidelities $10^{-3}$, $3\times10^{-4}$ and
$10^{-4}$; the pruned 98-parameter UCCSD needs three times more, the
two-local refinement path four to seven times more, and the projector path
sixteen times more, almost entirely in the coarse term conditioned on all
antibonding qubits.
\textbf{Conclusions:} Lattice refinement of the Hubbard model is a
validated, optimizer-free and measurement-free route to the fine ground
state whose convergence is governed by a symmetry-protected gap, and the
symmetry diagnostics identify in advance the coarse states that cannot be
refined.  At this size it is not a gate saver, however: an adiabatic ramp that
switches on the interaction from the free-fermion determinant reaches the
same accuracy at a fraction of the cost.  The comparison fixes the baseline that larger lattices, where the
free-fermion overlap decays, must beat.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================
In Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026} we implemented, end to end and at the gate
level, a pipeline for eigenstate preparation in which an eigenstate of a
coarse Hamiltonian is lifted by a prolongation operator into a finer model
space, transported adiabatically along a shift-controlled, gapped path
(\emph{resolution refinement}~\cite{Bogner2026}) and then cleaned by the
rodeo algorithm~\cite{Choi2021,Qian2024}, a projective spectral filter.  The
testbed there was the constant-pairing Hamiltonian, exactly solvable by the
Richardson ansatz, with ``resolution'' meaning the number of single-particle
levels at fixed particle number, and the assessment was sober: at eight
qubits the refinement stage was tied with simply filtering the
Hartree--Fock determinant, cost $2.5$--$3$ times an ordinary adiabatic ramp
from the Hartree--Fock Hamiltonian at every target accuracy, and was beaten
by a four-parameter pair-rotation ansatz; its merits were the absence of a
classical optimization loop and of training measurements.  A seniority-breaking
particle-hole interaction, studied in the companion paper~\cite{PHcompanion},
left the structure and convergence of the pipeline unchanged while raising the
depth of a Trotter step ninefold.

The present paper carries the same pipeline, the same benchmarks and the
same cost accounting to the Hubbard model~\cite{Hubbard1963,Arovas2022},
\begin{equation}
  H=-\sum_{\langle ij\rangle\sigma}t_{ij}\big(c^\dagger_{i\sigma}c_{j\sigma}+\mathrm{h.c.}\big)
   +U\sum_i n_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow},
  \label{eq:hubbard}
\end{equation}
for three reasons.  First, the Hubbard model is the canonical strongly
correlated fermion problem and the most frequently used benchmark for
quantum simulation of
fermions~\cite{Wecker2015,Cade2020,Cai2020,Stanisic2022}; it contains the
one-body particle-hole channel (hopping) that the pairing model lacks and
the two-body on-site term in their simplest forms, so it tests the lesson of
Ref.~\cite{PHcompanion} on a model where the seniority symmetry is absent from
the outset.  Second, it is the setting for which resolution refinement was
proposed~\cite{Bogner2026}: a \emph{lattice} refinement in which every coarse
site is resolved into a block of fine sites, the prolongation is a product of
Givens rotations rather than the gate-free embedding of the basis refinement,
and the fine lattice is the natural discretization of a continuum problem.
Third, the model is not exactly solvable in general, so the
exact-diagonalization (ED) reference that anchored every number in
Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026} must now be produced numerically; we keep that
discipline and benchmark every stage against ED in the conserved
$(N_\uparrow,N_\downarrow)$ sector with the dense and Lanczos solvers of our ED
code~\cite{HubbardNotebook}, which also covers the $t$--$J$ model and the
chain, square and checkerboard lattices.

Two structural results make the lattice refinement of the Hubbard model
particularly clean.  The Galerkin projection of the fine Hubbard Hamiltonian
onto the prolonged subspace is itself a Hubbard model, with $(t,U)\to(t/2,U/2)$
in one dimension --- so the hierarchy of resolutions is self-similar at
fixed $U/t$ --- and $(t,U)\to(t/2,U/4)$ in two dimensions
(Sec.~\ref{sec:galerkin}).  And the interpolation path conserves total spin
and the point-group symmetry of the lattice, so the gap that controls the
adiabatic transport is the distance to the next state \emph{in the symmetry
sector of the target}, which stays of order $t$ while the sector gap of the
fine Hamiltonian collapses with $U$ and with system size
(Sec.~\ref{sec:symmetry}); the converse is that a coarse ground state in the
wrong representation cannot be refined at all (Sec.~\ref{sec:2d}).

The paper follows the structure of Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026}.
Section~\ref{sec:model} fixes the model, the sector encoding, the ED
reference and the symmetry diagnostics.  Section~\ref{sec:pipeline} defines
the lattice prolongation, the Galerkin coarse Hamiltonian, the two refinement
paths, the rodeo filter and the variational comparator.
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the sector-exact results for the open chain
($2\to4\to8\to16$ sites) and the square plaquette ($2\times2\to4\times4$).
Section~\ref{sec:circuits} gives the Pauli structure of the Hubbard chain in
the Jordan--Wigner encoding, the cost of the prolongation and of the embedded
coarse term, the Trotterized refinement, the ordinary adiabatic ramp and the
pruned variational ansatz that serve as baselines, and the matched-observable
gate budgets with their minimum cost at fixed accuracy.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the results and the outlook.  All
numbers are produced by a reproducible notebook built on a tested library
(Sec.~\ref{sec:code}).

\section{The Hubbard model, its ED reference and the symmetry diagnostics}
\label{sec:model}
% ===========================================================================
We use $t_{ij}=t=1$ on nearest-neighbor bonds, open boundary conditions
throughout, the chain of $L$ sites and the $L\times L$ square lattice with
site index $i=xL+y$.  The Hamiltonian conserves $N_\uparrow$ and
$N_\downarrow$ separately, and all calculations are done in one such sector
of dimension $\binom{N}{N_\uparrow}\binom{N}{N_\downarrow}$.

\paragraph{Encoding and ED.}
Spin-orbitals are Jordan--Wigner ordered in two blocks, the $N$ up orbitals
followed by the $N$ down orbitals.  With this ordering a down hop picks up
the parity of all up electrons twice, which cancels, so the sector
Hamiltonian factorizes exactly as
\begin{equation}
  H=K_\uparrow\otimes\mathbb 1+\mathbb 1\otimes K_\downarrow
   +U\,\mathrm{diag}(\text{double occupancy}),
  \label{eq:kron}
\end{equation}
where $K_\sigma$ is the one-species hopping matrix on the sorted occupation
masks of that species.  Reference energies and eigenvectors are obtained
with dense diagonalization up to a few thousand states and with ARPACK
Lanczos beyond.  The builder is validated against free fermions at $U=0$,
the analytic dimer $E_0=\tfrac12(U-\sqrt{U^2+16t^2})$, and the term
decomposition used for product formulas in Sec.~\ref{sec:circuits}; the
sector dimensions encountered below range from $1$ (the filled dimer) to
$14\,400$ (four particles on sixteen sites).

\paragraph{Symmetry diagnostics.}
Three operators are built in the same sector basis and used to label every
eigenstate.  The total spin follows from
$\expval{\mathbf S^2}=S_z(S_z+1)+\|S^+\psi\|^2$, with
$S^+=\sum_ic^\dagger_{i\uparrow}c_{i\downarrow}$ applied as a map from the
$(N_\uparrow,N_\downarrow)$ to the $(N_\uparrow+1,N_\downarrow-1)$ sector with
the Jordan--Wigner signs of the block ordering.  A lattice symmetry is a
fermionic site permutation $c^\dagger_i\to c^\dagger_{\pi(i)}$, i.e.\ a signed
permutation matrix on each spin species; for the open chain this gives the
reflection parity, and for the open square the eight elements of $C_{4v}$,
from which the weight of a state in each irreducible representation follows
from the character formula
$w_\Gamma=(d_\Gamma/8)\sum_g\chi_\Gamma(g)^*\expval{g}{\psi}$.

\paragraph{Why the Hubbard model after the pairing model.}
In the pairing model the seniority symmetry confines the ground state to a
subspace in which the UCCSD solver is a doubles theory and the refinement
path has large gaps; the particle-hole term of Ref.~\cite{PHcompanion} broke
that symmetry by hand.  In Eq.~\eqref{eq:hubbard} the hopping term is a
one-body particle-hole operator from the outset, single excitations do not
vanish, and the only symmetries left to the pipeline are the ones it inherits
from the lattice: particle number per spin, total spin $S$, and the point
group.  These are exactly the symmetries that, as we show, decide which gap
governs the refinement.

\section{The pipeline for lattice refinement}
\label{sec:pipeline}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Prolongation: coarse sites to blocks of fine sites}
\label{sec:prolong}
A refinement step maps a coarse lattice of $N_c$ sites onto a fine lattice of
$N_f=bN_c$ sites by resolving every coarse site $c$ into a block $B(c)$ of
$b$ fine sites, $b=2$ for the chain (sites $2c,2c+1$) and $b=4$ for the
square lattice ($2\times2$ plaquettes).  The prolongation sends the coarse
orbital to the normalized symmetric (bonding) combination of its block,
\begin{equation}
  c^\dagger_{c\sigma}\;\longmapsto\;
  s^\dagger_{c\sigma}=\frac{1}{\sqrt b}\sum_{f\in B(c)}c^\dagger_{f\sigma},
  \label{eq:bonding}
\end{equation}
and a coarse Slater determinant to the corresponding product of
$s^\dagger$ operators; particle numbers are unchanged, so the filling halves
(quarters) at each step, as it must for a sequence of lattices approaching
the continuum at fixed particle number~\cite{Bogner2026}.  The map
$P$ is an isometry, $P^\dagger P=\mathbb 1$, from the coarse sector of
dimension $\binom{N_c}{N_\uparrow}\binom{N_c}{N_\downarrow}$ into the fine
sector; its complement is spanned by configurations in which at least one
antibonding block mode (and, for $b=4$, one of the two further orthogonal
block modes) is occupied.  On a quantum register in which the fine qubits of a
block are adjacent, $P$ is implemented by preparing the coarse state on one
qubit per block per spin and applying one Givens rotation per block per spin
--- two CNOTs each, i.e.\ $16$ CNOTs for $4\to8$ --- in contrast to the
gate-free embedding of the basis refinement.  In the sector basis $P$ is built
explicitly: for each coarse occupation pattern the fine amplitudes are
$b^{-n_\sigma/2}$ on every choice of one fine site per occupied block, with
the sign of the permutation that sorts the chosen fine indices.  Both
$P^\dagger P=\mathbb1$ and the identities below are verified to machine
precision in the notebook.

\subsection{The Galerkin coarse Hamiltonian}
\label{sec:galerkin}
The coarse Hamiltonian whose eigenstate is to be refined is the fine
Hamiltonian seen at coarse resolution, $\Hlow=P^\dagger\Hhigh P$.  With this
choice the identity $\expval{\Hhigh}{P\psi_{\rm low}}=E_{\rm low}$ holds by
construction for the exact coarse eigenstate, as in
Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026}; it says nothing about the overlap $p_0$ of
the prolonged state with the fine ground state, which must be computed, and
it is lost when the coarse state is itself an approximation with infidelity
$\epsilon_{\rm low}$ (a variational coarse solver, or the output of a
previous refinement step).  In that case the coarse error propagates through
the pipeline as in Sec.~III of Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026}: the final
infidelity is bounded by $(\sqrt{\epsilon_{\rm low}}+a_{\rm ad}+\eta)^2$,
with $a_{\rm ad}$ the adiabatic leakage of the transport and $\eta$ the
product-formula error, and Secs.~\ref{sec:dimer} and \ref{sec:res-uccsd}
show that in practice the coarse admixture is transported almost unchanged
and caps the refined overlap at roughly the coarse fidelity.  The Galerkin
model has a closed form.  Projecting the fine hopping term with Eq.~\eqref{eq:bonding}:
an intra-block bond contributes $-t(n_s-n_a)$, i.e.\ a constant $-t$ per
particle on the prolonged subspace, and each inter-block bond contributes
$\tfrac12 s^\dagger_c s_{c'}$; projecting the on-site term,
$n_{f\sigma}\to n_{s_c\sigma}/b$ for every $f\in B(c)$, gives
$U\sum_{f\in B(c)}n_{f\uparrow}n_{f\downarrow}\to(U/b)\,n_{s_c\uparrow}n_{s_c\downarrow}$.
Hence
\begin{align}
  \text{chain:}\quad &\Hlow=H_{\rm Hubbard}\!\big(L;\,t/2,\,U/2\big)-tN, \label{eq:gal1d}\\
  \text{square:}\quad&\Hlow=H_{\rm Hubbard}\!\big(L\times L;\,t/2,\,U/4\big)-2tN, \label{eq:gal2d}
\end{align}
with $N=N_\uparrow+N_\downarrow$, and with the same boundary conditions as the
fine lattice.  In one dimension the ratio $U/t$ is preserved, so a chain of
refinements $L\to2L\to4L\to\cdots$ is a sequence of Hubbard chains with the
same interaction strength and a hopping scale that doubles at each step: the
hierarchy is self-similar, and the coarsest member can be the two-site dimer
or, for $N=2L$, the filled dimer, a single computational basis state.  In
two dimensions the effective interaction of the coarse model is weaker by a
factor of two relative to its hopping.  We note that the Galerkin pair
$(\Hlow,\Hhigh)$ is a choice; a continuum-scaling pair ($t\propto a^{-2}$,
$U\propto a^{-1}$ in one dimension) would start the path from a different
coarse ground state and change only the initial overlap $p_0$, not the
construction.

\subsection{Two refinement paths}
\label{sec:paths}
\paragraph{Path A: the shift-controlled projector path.}
As in Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026},
\begin{align}
  H_A(s)={}&\cos^2\!\big(\tfrac{\pi s}{2}\big)P(\Hlow-\mu)P^\dagger
        +\sin^2\!\big(\tfrac{\pi s}{2}\big)(\Hhigh-\mu),\nonumber\\
        &s=t/T,
  \label{eq:pathA}
\end{align}
with the shift $\mu=E_{\rm low}+\mu_{\rm buf}$ placing the complement of the
prolonged subspace (where $P(\Hlow-\mu)P^\dagger$ vanishes) at energy
$\mu_{\rm buf}$ above the tracked state at $s=0$.  Because
$P(\Hlow-\mu)P^\dagger=\Pi_a(\Hhigh-\mu)\Pi_a$ with $\Pi_a$ the projector onto
empty antibonding modes, the coarse term is, on a device, the fine
Hamiltonian in the rotated (bonding/antibonding) frame conditioned on all
antibonding qubits being in $\ket{0}$ --- the multi-controlled structure whose
cost dominated the $3\to4$ chain of Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026}, now with
$2N_c$ control qubits.

\paragraph{Path B: adiabatic un-dimerization.}
The same end points are connected by a path that stays two-local in the
laboratory frame: add to the fine Hamiltonian an extra hopping $\Lambda$ on
the intra-block bonds and switch it off,
\begin{equation}
  H_B(s)=\Hhigh-\Lambda\cos^2\!\big(\tfrac{\pi s}{2}\big)
  \sum_{\langle ff'\rangle\in\mathrm{intra}}\sum_\sigma
  \big(c^\dagger_{f\sigma}c_{f'\sigma}+\mathrm{h.c.}\big).
  \label{eq:pathB}
\end{equation}
At $s=0$ the antibonding modes are pushed up by $2\Lambda$, and for
$\Lambda\to\infty$ the ground state of $H_B(0)$ is the prolonged Galerkin
ground state; at finite $\Lambda$ it differs from it by an admixture of
order $t/\Lambda$, so a transport that starts from the prolonged state
carries a residual $1-p\simeq c\,(t/\Lambda)^2$ to the end.  Path~B is the
dimerized Hubbard chain (the plaquette Hubbard model in two dimensions)
being adiabatically made uniform; every Trotter step along it costs what a
step of the fine Hamiltonian costs, with no conditioning, and the extra term
only rescales the angles of the intra-block blocks.

\paragraph{Refinement time.}
The exact evolution along either path is computed as in
Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026}: the $s$ interval is split into $M_s=96$
slices, the Hamiltonian at the slice midpoint is diagonalized once, and the
slice propagators are reused for every $T$.  We report the overlap
$p(T)=|\langle\Psi_{\rm high}|\psi(T)\rangle|^2$ with the exact fine ground
state on a grid of $T$ and quote $T^*$ as the first grid value with
$1-p(T)<10^{-3}$.  For the $14\,400$-dimensional sectors the slice
propagators are applied with a sparse Krylov exponential (48 slices).  As in
Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026} the pipeline is optimizer-free in execution but
not parameter-free: the refinement time $T$, the shift $\mu_{\rm buf}$ (or
the penalty $\Lambda$), the number of product-formula steps $n_s$, the filter
step $\delta t$ and the objective energy $\Eobj$ are all chosen here with
knowledge of the exact answer, and a device-level calibration protocol for
them is part of the open work (Sec.~\ref{sec:discussion}).

\subsection{The rodeo filter}
\label{sec:rodeo}
The filter is unchanged from Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026}: $M$ cycles of
the controlled evolution $e^{-i\Hhigh t_k}$ on one reused ancilla with the
phase $e^{i\Eobj t_k}$, Gaussian cycle times of RMS width $\sigma$, and
post-selection on the ancilla.  For an input
$\ket\psi=\sum_kc_k\ket{E_k}$ and $\Eobj=E_0$ the fidelity of the ensemble
of accepted runs obeys exactly
\begin{align}
  1-\mathcal F_M&=\frac{\sum_{k\neq0}|c_k|^2q_k^M}{p+\sum_{k\neq0}|c_k|^2q_k^M},\nonumber\\
  q_k&=\tfrac12\big(1+e^{-(E_k-E_0)^2\sigma^2/2}\big),
  \label{eq:fid}
\end{align}
which we evaluate from the ED spectrum and quote as the reference value
throughout.  Equation~\eqref{eq:fid} is the average over the Gaussian
ensemble of cycle times (drawn afresh for every attempt) with exact
controlled evolution and $\Eobj$ on resonance; the factor $q_k^M\ge2^{-M}$
makes $2^{-M}$ a lower bound on the suppression of each excited component,
not a rate that a single schedule attains, and a sampled average over a
finite number of time sets, as in the circuit-level study of
Sec.~\ref{sec:budget}, scatters around Eq.~\eqref{eq:fid} with a relative
standard error that grows with $M$.  The width is set by the smallest
excitation energy that carries weight in the input,
$\sigma=5/\Deff$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:symmetry}); choosing it from the bare sector
gap would lengthen the controlled evolutions by a factor of up to three for
no gain.

\subsection{The variational comparator}
\label{sec:uccsd}
As in Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026} the pipeline is compared with a unitary
coupled-cluster singles-and-doubles (UCCSD) preparation.  The reference is
the free-fermion determinant (the $N_\sigma$ lowest orbitals of the hopping
matrix of the lattice), the pool contains the spin-resolved singles and
doubles from it, and the state is the factorized (disentangled) product of
exponentials $\prod_\mu e^{\theta_\mu(\tau_\mu-\tau_\mu^\dagger)}\ket{\rm HF}$
in a fixed order --- all singles first, then the doubles, each class in
lexicographic order of its orbital labels --- optimized by L-BFGS with the
analytic (adjoint) gradient; a different ordering is a different ansatz.  The simulation is
exact for the ansatz: the sector is transformed to the orbital occupation
basis, where every generator connects pairs of determinants and its
exponential is the Givens-type rotation
$1+\sin\theta\,K+(\cos\theta-1)\Pi$, $\Pi=-K^2$, the same structure as the
compiled excitation blocks of Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026}.  On a device the
reference and the excitations live on the site register after a Givens
network ($N(N-1)/2$ rotations per spin), and we count the circuit with the
Pauli-rotation primitive of Sec.~\ref{sec:circuits}: a single compiles into
two strings, a double into eight, and a weight-$w$ string costs $2(w-1)$ CNOTs.
The comparator is used in two ways, as the coarse solver on the four-site
chain (26 parameters, 8 qubits), whose state is then prolonged and refined,
and as the direct competitor on the eight-site chain (198 parameters, 16
qubits).  Since most of the 198 amplitudes are small, we also report the
\emph{pruned} ansatz obtained by keeping the operators whose converged
amplitude exceeds $10^{-3}$ and re-optimizing: it is the demonstrated
restricted ansatz that an adaptive construction such as ADAPT-VQE would at
best find, and it is what the gate budget of Sec.~\ref{sec:budget} should be
compared with.  Two further baselines are defined there: the free-fermion
determinant itself, and the ordinary adiabatic ramp that switches on $U$
from it.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{The open chain refined from four to eight sites}
\label{sec:chain48}
Table~\ref{tab:chain48} and Fig.~\ref{fig:chain48} summarize the refinement
of the four-site chain with $N_\uparrow=N_\downarrow=2$ (half filling, 36
states) to the eight-site chain (quarter filling, 784 states) for
$U/t=0,2,4,8$.  Three features stand out.  (i) The prolonged state is a good
start at every coupling, $p_0=0.75$--$0.81$, with a weak maximum at $U=2t$;
unlike the pairing model, the free-fermion case is not trivial, because the
bonding modes are not the lowest fine single-particle orbitals.  (ii) The
sector gap of the fine Hamiltonian falls by a factor of four over the range
of $U$, from $0.53t$ to $0.14t$, but the refinement time does not move: with
$\mu_{\rm buf}=2t$ the overlap reaches $p>0.997$ at $T=8/t$,
$1-p<10^{-3}$ at $T^*=10/t$ and $p>0.9999$ at $T=16/t$ for all four
couplings, and with $\mu_{\rm buf}=t$ the same values are reached at roughly
twice the time.  The minimum of the sector gap along Path~A sits at $s=1$
(it is the fine-sector gap itself) in every case, so the global path gap
cannot be the quantity that controls the transport; the reason is the symmetry
protection discussed next.  (iii) Path~B refines at a comparable rate but
saturates at the finite-$\Lambda$ ceiling: at $T=64/t$,
$1-p=4.8$--$7.3\times10^{-3}$ for $\Lambda=8t$ and $1.7$--$3.7\times10^{-3}$
for $\Lambda=16t$, with a weak and non-monotonic $U$ dependence, consistent
with the $(t/\Lambda)^2$ scaling of the admixture (the overlap of the

the transport, limits Path~B; a schedule with $\Lambda\to\infty$ at $s=0$ or
a final filter removes it.

\subsection{Which gap controls the refinement: symmetry protection}
\label{sec:symmetry}
Both terms of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pathA} commute with the total spin and with
the point group of the fine lattice (the block structure respects the
reflection of the chain and the $C_{4v}$ group of the square), and the
prolongation maps irreducible representations onto themselves.  The
transported state therefore never leaves the symmetry sector of the coarse
ground state --- a spin singlet, even under reflection, for the cases of
Table~\ref{tab:chain48} --- and the states that can be excited along the
path are only those of the same sector.  Figure~\ref{fig:weights} makes this
explicit with the symmetry labels of Sec.~\ref{sec:model}: for $U=4t$ the
lowest fine excitations at $0.223t$ (a reflection-odd triplet) and $0.459t$
(an even triplet) carry weights of order $10^{-31}$, and the first state with
nonzero weight ($4.6\times10^{-5}$), an even singlet, lies at $\Deff=0.588t$;
at $U=8t$ the corresponding numbers are $0.137t$ and $0.286t$ (triplets,
zero weight) and $\Deff=0.366t$ (weight $3\times10^{-7}$), with the bulk of
the excited weight lying still higher.  The weight of the prolonged state in
the even-singlet sector is one to twelve digits for every $U>0$.  $\Deff$
falls with $U$ much more slowly than $\Delta$, which explains the
$U$-independent $T^*$ of Table~\ref{tab:chain48}.

Figure~\ref{fig:gaps} follows the two gaps along Path~A: the sector gap
and the gap to the lowest state with the same $(S,\text{parity})$ as the
tracked state.  The latter never approaches the former; its minimum is
$\Deff$ itself, at $s=1$.  This is the isolated-branch condition of the
adiabatic theorem verified numerically along the whole path, within the
symmetry sector in which the transport actually takes place, rather than
assumed.  Table~\ref{tab:bound} evaluates the sufficient
adiabatic time of Jansen, Ruskai and Seiler~\cite{Jansen2007} in the form
used in Sec.~IV.E of Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026},
$T_b=\epsilon^{-1/2}\int_0^1[\|\ddot H\|/\Delta^2+7\|\dot H\|^2/\Delta^3]\,ds$
with $\|B-A\|$ the spectral norm of $\Hhigh-P\Hlow P^\dagger$ in the sector,
once with the sector gap and once with the symmetry-resolved gap.  The
symmetry-resolved bound is smaller by a factor $5$ to $17$ but remains three
to five orders of magnitude above the empirical $T^*=10/t$, as the pairing
bound did (there $3\times10^2$); the larger ratio here reflects
$\|B-A\|=11$--$20$, set by the shift $\mu$ and the width of the Hubbard
spectrum.  As in Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026} the two numbers answer
different questions --- the bound guarantees a worst-case operator-norm
error for every initial state in the branch, $T^*$ records one overlap for
one initial state --- so the ratio measures the slack of the criterion, not
an error in either.

The first-order response norm of the path,
$\chi_u(s)=\sum_{n>0}|\langle n(s)|\partial_uH|0(s)\rangle|^2/[E_n(s)-E_0(s)]^2$
with $u=\sin^2(\pi s/2)$ and $\partial_uH=(\Hhigh-\mu)-P(\Hlow-\mu)P^\dagger$,
is the quantity whose integral along the path sets the adiabatic leakage at
leading order; for the pairing model Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026} obtained
it in closed form at the start of the path, $\chi_u(0)=g^2/\mu_{\rm buf}^2$.
Figure~\ref{fig:ramp} (left) follows it along Path~A for the chain $4\to8$:
$\chi_u(0)=0.375$, $0.527$, $0.715$ and $0.874$ for $U/t=0,2,4,8$, the
maximum sits at the initial endpoint for every coupling, and the norm
falls by a factor two to four along the path (at $U=8t$ it rises again
toward $s=1$, to $0.45$, as $\Deff$ shrinks).  The shift fixes the
initial value as it did for the pairing model, and the growth of
$\chi_u(0)$ with $U$ is modest, in line with the flat $T^*$.

\subsection{Bootstrapping from the filled dimer and composing steps}
\label{sec:dimer}
The self-similarity of the one-dimensional hierarchy allows the chain of
refinements to start from the two-site dimer with $N_\uparrow=N_\downarrow=2$,
whose sector contains a single state --- both sites doubly occupied ---
with coarse energy $E_{\rm low}=2\cdot(U/2)-2t\cdot2=U-4t$.  This is the
analog of the $k=2$ determinant that seeded the $k=2\to4$ chain in
Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026}, and it requires no classical or variational
preprocessing.  Table~\ref{tab:dimer} shows the step $2\to4$: the
doubly-occupied seed is, as expected, a worse start as $U$ grows,
$p_0=0.80$ at $U=0$ down to $0.48$ at $U=8t$, but the effective gap is
large ($\Deff=2.2t$--$0.8t$) and $p>0.9997$ is reached by $T=16/t$ at every
$U$.

Figure~\ref{fig:composed} composes the steps.  With the \emph{refined}
four-site state (not the exact one) as the input of the $4\to8$ step and the
shift referenced to its variational energy
$\expval{\Hlow}{\psi}$, as in the $3\to4$ chain of
Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026}, the composed chain $2\to4\to8$ at $U=4t$ with
$T=16/t$ per step reaches $p=0.99988$, against $0.99994$ from the exact
coarse input; with $T=8/t$ in the first step the second step saturates at
$p=0.9979$, the overlap of its input, because the admixture of other coarse
states refines to other fine states.  The composed chain is also better than
a single-shot refinement $2\to8$ in which each coarse site is resolved into a
block of four at once: that start has $p_0=0.579$ and reaches $p=0.9966$ at
$T=16/t$ and $0.99988$ only at $T=32/t$.  Two steps of $T=16/t$ therefore
take a single computational basis state to the eight-site ground state with
$p>0.9998$ before any filtering.

\subsection{Eight to sixteen sites}
\label{sec:chain816}
The protection survives a doubling of the system.  Refining the eight-site
chain to sixteen sites at $(2,2)$ ($784\to14\,400$ states, $U=4t$) starts
from $p_0=0.869$, with $E_{\rm low}=-6.817213$ and $E_{\rm high}=-7.209813$,
faces a fine-sector gap of only $0.051t$, and still reaches $p=0.9924$ at
$T=8/t$, $0.99931$ at $T=16/t$ and $0.99995$ at $T=32/t$.  Among the forty
lowest eigenstates of the fine sector the first to carry weight lies at
$\Deff=0.138t$ (weight $10^{-4}$), the next at $0.415t$ and $0.505t$ (the
latter with weight $10^{-2}$), while the eight other states below $0.4t$,
including the one that defines the sector gap, carry none.  Composing three steps from the filled dimer,
$2\to4\to8\to16$ with $T=16/t$ each and the refined state as the input of
every step, gives $p=0.99923$ for the sixteen-site ground state.

\subsection{Two dimensions: the plaquette and the wrong symmetry sector}
\label{sec:2d}
For the square lattice the first step is $2\times2\to4\times4$.  With one
particle per spin ($16\to256$ states) the refinement behaves as in one
dimension: both ground states are $A_1$, $p_0=0.80$--$0.85$ for $U/t=0,4,8$,
$\Deff\simeq2.1t$--$2.2t$, and $p>0.9997$ at $T=8/t$, $p>0.99998$ at
$T=16/t$ (Table~\ref{tab:2d}).

With two particles per spin ($36\to14\,400$ states) the prolonged coarse
ground state has \emph{zero} overlap with the fine ground state, and the
symmetry diagnostics of Sec.~\ref{sec:model} identify why
(Fig.~\ref{fig:2d}).  The half-filled coarse plaquette (Galerkin parameters
$t/2$, $U/4$) has, for every $U>0$, a $B_1$ singlet ground state with an
$A_2$ triplet close above it ($0.071t$ at $U=4t$, $0.15t$ at $8t$); the
dilute fine lattice --- four electrons on sixteen open sites --- has an
$A_2$ \emph{triplet} ground state for every $U>0$ studied
($U/t=1,2,4,6,8$), the $B_1$ singlet lying $0.04t$--$0.11t$ above it.  The
prolongation commutes with $\mathbf S^2$ and with $C_{4v}$, so the two cannot
be connected: the decomposition of the fine ground state over the prolonged
coarse eigenstates gives weight $0$ on the coarse ground state, $0.647$ on
the $A_2$ triplet, and $0.007$ on all other coarse states together ($U=4t$).
Refining that triplet along Path~A ($\mu=E_1^{\rm low}+2t$) reaches
$p=0.99944$ at $T=8/t$, $0.99997$ at $T=16/t$ and $0.99999$ at $T=32/t$,
although the fine-sector gap is only $0.090t$; the lowest state of the
$(A_2,S=1)$ sector that carries weight lies $1.77t$ above the ground state
(among the thirty lowest eigenstates), so once the right sector is chosen
the symmetry protection of Sec.~\ref{sec:symmetry} works strongly in its
favor.  The same path started from the coarse ground state gives
$p=3\times10^{-27}$.

Two conclusions follow for the method.  The coarse state to be refined is
the lowest coarse eigenstate \emph{in the symmetry sector of the target},
which need not be the coarse ground state, and the coarse solver (UCCSD or
otherwise) must therefore be run with the target's quantum numbers imposed
or at least diagnosed; and the rodeo filter cannot rescue a wrong choice,
since a state of zero overlap has nothing to amplify.  Conversely, when the
coarse ground state is in the wrong sector the refinement becomes a sensitive
detector of a level crossing between the coarse and the fine problem --- here
between the singlet and the triplet as the filling goes from one half to one
eighth, a crossing that persists over the whole range of $U$ studied rather
than being tuned by it.  \todo{The $U=0$ point is degenerate in both models
and its labels are not meaningful; consider adding the $(S,\Gamma)$-resolved
spectrum of the fine lattice at a second filling, e.g.\ $(3,3)$, and the
periodic lattice, where Lieb's theorem fixes the spin at half filling.}

\subsection{Rodeo filtering of the refined state}
\label{sec:res-rodeo}
With $\sigma=5/\Deff$ ($5.7/t$ to $13.6/t$ for $U/t=0$ to $8$) the filter
applied to the prolonged state behaves as the pairing case did
(Fig.~\ref{fig:rodeo}): the infidelity falls from $1-p_0\simeq0.2$ to
$4$--$5\times10^{-3}$ after six cycles and $2$--$3\times10^{-4}$ after ten,
the geometric suppression of Eq.~\eqref{eq:fid} (at least $2^{-M}$ per
excited component), with an ensemble acceptance that converges to $p_0$.  Applied to the Path-A state refined for
$T=16/t$ the same filter starts from $1-p\simeq10^{-4}$ and reaches
$0.5$--$1.4\times10^{-6}$ after six cycles; applied to the Path-B state
($\Lambda=16t$, $T=32/t$) it starts from the ceiling, $1-p\simeq2\times10^{-3}$,
and reaches $2$--$4\times10^{-5}$ after six cycles; applied to the eight-site
UCCSD state it starts from $1-p=4.7\times10^{-4}$, $3.1\times10^{-3}$ and
$1.2\times10^{-2}$ at $U/t=2,4,8$ (zero at $U=0$, where the free-fermion
reference is exact) and reaches $7\times10^{-6}$, $5\times10^{-5}$ and
$2\times10^{-4}$.  As in Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026}, the question that
matters is not whether the refined input converges faster per cycle --- it
does, by the ratio of the initial infidelities --- but whether the cycles
saved pay for the refinement gates; that comparison requires the
circuit-level Trotterization of Sec.~\ref{sec:circuits}.

\subsection{The UCCSD comparator}
\label{sec:res-uccsd}
Table~\ref{tab:uccsd} collects the variational results.  On the coarse chain
the 26-parameter UCCSD is exact at $U=0$ and reaches infidelities
$1.8\times10^{-4}$, $3.3\times10^{-3}$ and $1.7\times10^{-2}$ at
$U/t=2,4,8$ from a free-fermion reference that is very poor at strong
coupling ($E_{\rm HF}-E_{\rm FCI}=2.3t$ at $U=8t$); on the fine chain the
198-parameter ansatz does slightly better, $4.7\times10^{-4}$,
$3.1\times10^{-3}$, $1.2\times10^{-2}$, at a cost of $3.1\times10^4$ CNOTs
against $1.7\times10^3$.  Used as the coarse solver, the UCCSD state is
prolonged with essentially the same $p_0$ as the exact coarse state
($0.800$ against $0.797$ at $U=4t$), but the refinement then saturates at
$p=0.99669$ ($U=4t$) and $0.98234$ ($U=8t$) instead of $0.9999$: as in the
composed chain of Sec.~\ref{sec:dimer}, the admixture of other coarse
eigenstates in the variational input is transported to other fine
eigenstates, and the refined overlap is capped at roughly the coarse
fidelity.  The coarse solver must therefore be converged to the accuracy
required of the final state, or the filter must do the rest.

Most of the fine-chain amplitudes are small: at $U=4t$, $72$ of the $198$
exceed $10^{-2}$ and $98$ exceed $10^{-3}$.  Keeping those $98$ operators
($12$ singles and $86$ doubles) and re-optimizing from the converged values
gives $E=-5.618755$ and $1-F=3.145\times10^{-3}$ against $-5.618758$ and
$3.143\times10^{-3}$ for the full pool: the same accuracy at half the gate
count, $15\,120$ against $30\,864$ CNOTs.  This pruned ansatz is the
variational comparator of Sec.~\ref{sec:budget}; it is a demonstrated
restricted ansatz, not an adaptive selection, which would start from a
different (and possibly smaller) operator set.

\subsection{Pauli structure and step costs}
In the block Jordan--Wigner ordering of Sec.~\ref{sec:model} an open chain
of $L_f$ sites on $2L_f$ qubits has $4(L_f-1)$ hopping strings
$\tfrac t2(X_jX_{j+1}+Y_jY_{j+1})$ of weight two, $L_f$ interaction strings
$\tfrac U4Z_jZ_{j+L_f}$ and $2L_f$ single-$Z$ terms: $24$ strings for
$L_f=4$, which we verified against the full-space Pauli decomposition of the
library on eight qubits, and $52$ strings of weight $\le2$ for $L_f=8$,
against the $325$ strings of weight up to eight of the $k=4$ pairing model
on half the qubits.  With the Pauli-rotation primitive of
Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026} --- $2(w-1)$ CNOTs per weight-$w$ string, two
more per rotation when controlled --- a first-order Trotter step of the fine
chain costs $72$ CNOTs ($52$ rotations), a second-order step $144$, and a
controlled second-order step $352$.  The product formula groups the bonds
into two layers of disjoint bonds plus the diagonal interaction; each layer
is a product of commuting two-qubit blocks and is realized exactly, so the
Trotter error comes only from the non-commutativity between the three
groups, as on hardware.  The $Z_jZ_{j+L_f}$ terms are the only non-local
ones; the site-major ordering, which makes them adjacent at the price of
weight-three hopping strings, is the alternative to be compared.
\todo{Compare the two orderings on a linear-connectivity device.}

\paragraph{Prolongation and the coarse term.}
The prolongation $4\to8$ costs $16$ CNOTs.  The embedded coarse term of
Path~A is the coarse Hubbard step on the bonding register --- $24$
rotations, $32$ CNOTs unconditioned --- with every rotation conditioned on
the eight antibonding qubits being empty.  With the Gray-code
phase-polynomial convention that gave the $14$- and $350$-CNOT figures of
Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026} (which is exact for few controls but grows as
$2^{c+1}$) this is $1.2\times10^4$ CNOTs per application; with a
clean-ancilla ladder of relative-phase Toffolis ($6(c-1)+2$ extra CNOTs per
rotation) it is $1\,088$.  We use the latter, i.e.\ $7.6$ fine Trotter steps
per application of the coarse term, and note that either figure makes the
coarse term, not the fine step, the cost of Path~A.  \todo{Compile the
conditioned coarse term explicitly and verify it against the sector matrix
exponential, as was done for the $2\to4$ phase in paper I.}

\subsection{Trotterized refinement}
Figure~\ref{fig:trotter} replaces the exact slice propagation at $T=16/t$,
$U=4t$ by first- and second-order product formulas over the groups
$[P(\Hlow-\mu)P^\dagger,\,K_{\rm even},\,K_{\rm odd},\,D-\mu]$ for Path~A
and $[(1+\Lambda\cos^2)K_{\rm even},\,K_{\rm odd},\,D]$ for Path~B.  The
Hubbard path needs many more steps than the pairing path did: the
second-order formula reproduces the exact-slice overlap to within $10^{-3}$
with $n_s=128$ steps for Path~A ($p=0.99983$), $256$ for Path~B with
$\Lambda=8t$ ($0.99213$) and $512$ for $\Lambda=16t$ ($0.99342$), against
$43$ steps for $T^*=16$ in Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026}.  The reason is the
norm of the step generators, $\|B-A\|=14$ for Path~A and the enhanced
intra-block hopping $(1+\Lambda)$ for Path~B, which makes $\Lambda=8t$ the
better operating point of the two-local path despite its higher ceiling.
At these step counts the refinement costs $2.97\times10^5$ CNOTs for Path~A
(of which $2.8\times10^5$ in the coarse term), $3.7\times10^4$ for Path~B at
$\Lambda=8t$ and $7.4\times10^4$ at $\Lambda=16t$ (Table~\ref{tab:budget}).
First-order formulas do not reach the exact-slice overlap within $10^{-3}$
even at $512$ steps.

The counted circuit of Path~A does not exponentiate the embedded coarse
term as one block: on a device it is the coarse Hubbard step, i.e.\ the
three conditioned coarse groups $P(D_c-\mu)P^\dagger$, $PK^c_{\rm even}P^\dagger$
and $PK^c_{\rm odd}P^\dagger$, which do not commute.  Repeating the
second-order refinement with the coarse term split accordingly (six groups
in all) gives $p=0.997299$, $0.999818$, $0.999941$ and $0.999943$ at
$n_s=64$, $128$, $256$ and $512$, against $0.997456$, $0.999831$,
$0.999941$ and $0.999943$ with the coarse term as one block; the step
count $n_s=128$ and the cost of Path~A are unchanged, and the split-group
state is the one used below.

\paragraph{Baseline: the ordinary adiabatic ramp.}
The simplest adiabatic preparation of the fine ground state needs neither a
coarse model nor a prolongation: start from the free-fermion determinant
of the fine chain, prepared by a Givens network ($2\times28$ rotations,
$112$ CNOTs), and switch on the interaction,
$H_R(s)=K+\sin^2(\pi s/2)\,U\sum_in_{i\uparrow}n_{i\downarrow}$.  At
$U=4t$ the determinant has $p_0=0.7804$ --- essentially the overlap of the
prolonged state, $0.7974$ --- and the same $\Deff=0.588t$, since it lies in
the same symmetry sector.  Figure~\ref{fig:ramp} (right) compares the two
transports with exact slices: the ramp reaches $p=0.96878$, $0.99686$,
$0.99960$ and $0.99989$ at $T=4,8,12,16$ (and $0.99999$ at $24/t$), as fast
as Path~A and far above the ceiling of Path~B, with no shift and no
coarse term.  Its product-formula version is the fine-chain step with a
rescaled interaction angle: $64$ second-order steps reproduce the
exact-slice overlap at $T=8/t$ ($0.996891$ against $0.996861$) for
$112+64\times144=9\,328$ CNOTs, and $128$ steps at $T=16/t$ give
$0.999659$ (exact $0.999885$) for $18\,544$ CNOTs.  This is the baseline
that the refinement paths must beat, the analog of the Hartree--Fock
ramp of Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026}.



\subsection{Matched-observable comparison}
\label{sec:budget}
Following Sec.~V.C of Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026} we feed the same
Trotterized rodeo filter --- second order, fixed step $\delta t$,
$\sigma=5/\Deff=8.5/t$, forty Gaussian time sets drawn once and shared by
all inputs, up to six cycles --- eight inputs: the prolonged coarse ground
state, the free-fermion determinant, the pruned and the full UCCSD states,
the ramp states at $T=8/t$ and $16/t$, the Path-B state ($\Lambda=8t$,
$n_s=256$) and the Path-A state ($n_s=128$, coarse groups split), and
compare the acceptance-weighted ensemble infidelity of the accepted runs
against the CNOTs per attempt and per accepted preparation (input plus
filter, early termination after the first failed ancilla measurement, the
expectation of the cost taken over the time sets).  Two implementation
details differ from paper I and are forced by the Hubbard norms.  The fixed
step is diagonalized once, so that $n$ steps are applied exactly as
$We^{in\phi}W^\dagger$ and the cycle times are quantized to multiples of
$\delta t$ (on average $68$ steps per cycle, $2.4\times10^4$ CNOTs); the
filter then projects on the eigenvector of the step unitary closest to the
ground state, whose eigenphase is what the energy scan of the rodeo locates
on a device, and we take $\Eobj$ equal to it rather than to $E_0$.  The
Trotter floor of the filter is that eigenvector's infidelity with the exact
ground state: $2.6\times10^{-3}$ at $\delta t=0.25/t$ (the step of paper I,
where $\Eobj-E_0=0.09t$ would also have spoiled the acceptance),
$5.8\times10^{-5}$ at $\delta t=0.1/t$ and $3.5\times10^{-6}$ at $0.05/t$;
we operate at $\delta t=0.1/t$, where $\Eobj-E_0=0.013t$.  Because the
cycle times are quantized, every cycle shares the eigenvectors of the step
unitary, so the long-run state of the filter is that eigenvector itself
and, with $\Eobj$ on its eigenphase, the long-run acceptance tends to the
input's weight on it instead of decaying; this is the fixed-step analog
of the averaged post-selection map of paper I, whose dominant eigenoperator
was mixed and whose acceptance decayed at $0.2\%$ per cycle.  The sampled
infidelities carry bootstrap relative standard errors (over the forty time
sets) of up to $18\%$ for the unrefined inputs at $M=6$ and below $10\%$
for the refined and variational ones; the exact-ensemble values of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:fid} for the same inputs lie below the sampled ones once the
latter approach the Trotter floor, which Eq.~\eqref{eq:fid} does not
contain (for the ramp state at $T=16/t$: $5.3\times10^{-6}$ exact against
$6.2\times10^{-5}$ sampled at $M=6$).

for $10^{-3}$ without any filter cycle, $4.0\times10^4$ for

three.  The pruned UCCSD state costs $3.3$ and $2.9$ times more at the
first two targets and does not reach $10^{-4}$ within six cycles; the
full pool is a further $10$--$25\%$ more expensive for the same accuracy.
The two-local refinement path, Path~B at $\Lambda=8t$, costs $7.4$ and $4$
times the ramp, because its preparation is twice as expensive
($3.7\times10^4$ against $1.9\times10^4$ CNOTs) and starts twenty times
worse ($1-p=7.9\times10^{-3}$ against $3.4\times10^{-4}$) owing to the
finite-$\Lambda$ ceiling.  Path~A delivers the best unfiltered input
($1-p=1.8\times10^{-4}$) but at $3\times10^5$ CNOTs, sixteen times the
ramp, almost entirely in the conditioned coarse term; it is the only input
other than the ramp to reach $10^{-4}$, at $3.4\times10^5$ CNOTs.  The
prolonged state and the bare determinant, at $16$ and $112$ CNOTs, need
six cycles and $1.5$--$1.6\times10^5$ CNOTs to reach $5\times10^{-3}$, with
acceptances of $0.80$ and $0.78$, and reach none of the targets.  Only the
inputs that start within a decade of the floor --- the ramp at $T=16/t$ and
Path~A --- converge to it within six cycles; the others are still a factor
$1.5$ to $80$ above it at $M=6$, so the floor is set by $\delta t$ but
whether it is reached is set by the input.

Three qualifications apply as in paper I.  The costs are per accepted
preparation at the operating point of each input; the setup costs
--- the classical optimization and its measurements for UCCSD, the
calibration of $(T,\mu_{\rm buf},n_s)$ or of $(T,n_s)$ for the adiabatic
inputs, the energy scan that locates $\Eobj$ for the filter --- are not
included and are amortized over the preparations that use them.  The
refinement parameters are not optimized: $T=16/t$ and $\mu_{\rm buf}=2t$
are the first grid values that meet the accuracy criterion, and a joint
search over $(T,\mu_{\rm buf},\Lambda,n_s,\delta t)$ may move every curve
by a factor of order unity, but not the factor sixteen between Path~A and
the ramp, which is the cost of the conditioned coarse term, nor the
twenty-fold ceiling of Path~B.  And the comparison is at one size and one
coupling, where the free-fermion determinant has the same overlap and the
same $\Deff$ as the prolonged coarse state; on larger lattices and at
stronger coupling, where the free-fermion overlap with the interacting
ground state decays, the ramp loses its starting advantage while the
coarse eigenstate, which already contains the correlations at coarse
resolution, does not --- that is the regime in which the refinement can
pay, and it is not reached at sixteen qubits.  Refinement is therefore not
a gate saver on the Hubbard chain at this size either, and its merit
remains that it needs no classical optimizer, no training measurements
and no choice of ansatz.

\section{Discussion and outlook}
\label{sec:discussion}
% ===========================================================================
\paragraph{Lattice versus basis refinement.}
The lattice refinement of the Hubbard model is structurally cleaner than the
basis refinement of the pairing model: a Galerkin coarse model in closed
form, a self-similar hierarchy in one dimension, a two-local Hamiltonian
with $O(L_f)$ Pauli strings of weight two, a prolongation of $O(N_c)$ gates.
Against this stands a coarse term that is conditioned on $2N_c$ qubits, and
Path~B as its two-local replacement with a $(t/\Lambda)^2$ ceiling and a
step count that grows with $\Lambda$.  The Hubbard path also needs three to
ten times more Trotter steps than the pairing path for the same adiabatic
time, because the shifted generators have larger norms; this, more than the
adiabatic time itself, sets the budget.

\paragraph{What the matched comparison shows.}
The ordinary adiabatic ramp from the free-fermion determinant is the
cheapest route to every target accuracy at $U=4t$ on sixteen qubits, by a
factor of three over the pruned variational ansatz, four to seven over the
two-local refinement path and sixteen over the projector path.  The reason
is specific to the size: the determinant has the same overlap with the
target ($0.78$ against $0.80$) and the same symmetry-protected gap as the
prolonged coarse eigenstate, so the ramp, which pays only for fine-chain
steps, starts from the same place and travels at the same speed with
cheaper gates.  The case for refinement therefore rests on the regime
where the coarse eigenstate is a better start than the determinant ---
larger lattices and stronger coupling, where the free-fermion overlap
decays with size while the coarse state carries the correlations at coarse
resolution --- and that regime must be demonstrated, not assumed; the
present comparison fixes the baseline it has to beat.  Two further
lessons are inherited from paper I.  A conditioned coarse term is never
affordable: the two-local path, or a schedule that avoids the embedding
altogether, is the only form of the projector path worth compiling.  And
the figure of merit is the cost at fixed accuracy, per accepted
preparation, with the filter cycles included; at fixed cycle count every
input ``converges to the floor'' only in the sense that the best ones do.

\paragraph{Which gap matters.}
The quantity that controls the refinement is the symmetry-protected
effective gap $\Deff$, not the sector gap; this is why $T^*$ is flat in $U$
and in size here, why the sufficient time of the adiabatic theorem is a
closer (though still remote) estimate when evaluated with the symmetry-resolved
gap, and why $\Deff$ is the right width for the rodeo filter.
Ref.~\cite{EigenQPairing2026} found that its scan of the refinement time
against the shift-controlled gap did not identify a power law (two
exponents fit the four points equally well, and the response norm of the
path varied with the shift); the lesson here is that any such scan must be
made against the gap in the symmetry sector of the tracked state, with the
response norm held fixed, before an exponent can be read off.  Its
converse, the zero-overlap plaquette, is a warning for any refinement or
nucleation scheme: the coarse state must carry the quantum numbers of the
target, which the ED diagnostics of Sec.~\ref{sec:model} --- or, on a device,
measurements of $\mathbf S^2$ and of the point-group characters --- can
check before any gate is spent.  The singlet--triplet crossing between the
half-filled plaquette and the dilute $4\times4$ lattice is a property of the
filling, not of $U$, over the whole range studied.

\paragraph{Coarse accuracy.}
Composing steps with refined inputs, and starting from a variational coarse
state, both show the same ceiling: an admixture of other coarse eigenstates
in the input refines to other fine eigenstates and caps the final overlap
at roughly the input fidelity.  The coarse stage must therefore be as
accurate as the target, or the filter must pay for the difference; the
filled dimer, which is exact, is from this point of view the best coarse
state the hierarchy has.

\paragraph{Strong coupling.}
At fixed $U/t$ the hierarchy is self-similar, so refinement does not by
itself reach the $t$--$J$ regime; a path that raises $U$ along with the
resolution, or a refinement at fixed filling (prolonging with doubled
particle number, which is not a state map) are the options, and the ED
code's $t$--$J$ solver gives the reference.  The rodeo filter, in contrast,
applies unchanged to the $t$--$J$ Hamiltonian in its constrained sector.
\todo{Decide whether a $t$--$J$ section belongs in this paper.}

\paragraph{Relation to nucleation.}
In one dimension the dimer-seeded hierarchy and the nucleation of a chain
from a dimer~\cite{Sadia2026} start from the same state and reach the same
target by refining resolution versus growing the domain; the Hubbard chain
is the natural place to compare the two paths at matched gate count, and the
optimized variants of either (a warm-started variational path) are the
obvious way to cut the $10^2$--$10^3$ Trotter steps found here.

\paragraph{Hardware targets.}
The $4\to8$ chain (16 qubits) and the $2\times2\to4\times4$ plaquette at
$(1,1)$ (32 qubits, 256-dimensional sector) are within reach of current
devices; the Trotter step is shallow ($72$ CNOTs first order at $L_f=8$),
and Path~B needs nothing beyond the fine Hamiltonian's own blocks.
\todo{PBC/APBC variants; a schedule with $\Lambda\to\infty$ at $s=0$;
Path-B at $T<16$ since it saturates early.}

\paragraph{Open points.}
The refinement and filter parameters are calibrated against the exact
answer, as in paper I; a device-level protocol (an energy scan for
$\Eobj$, acceptance statistics for $\sigma$ and $\delta t$, a convergence
test in $T$ and $n_s$ on the measured energy) is the missing piece for a
hardware run.  The variational comparator is a demonstrated restricted
ansatz; an adaptive construction, and a comparison at matched shot budget
including the training measurements, are not done.  The size and coupling
dependence of the ramp-versus-refinement comparison --- the one
measurement that can turn the verdict --- requires the $8\to16$ chain at
the circuit level ($14\,400$ states, Krylov propagation) and is the natural
next step.
